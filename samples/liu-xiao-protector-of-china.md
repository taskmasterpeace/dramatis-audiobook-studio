# Liu Xiao: Protector of China

## Chapter One — The Rain on Wangfujing

The rain came early that evening, before Liu Xiao had finished his last client.

He felt it before he heard it — a shift in the air, a coolness that climbed up from the pavement and settled around his ankles, the particular hush that fell over Wangfujing when the first fat drops began to strike the awnings. The street sellers two stalls down started yanking their tarps tight. Somewhere a tour guide's loudspeaker squawked and went silent. Under Liu's hands, the shoulders of the German tourist tensed against the change, and Liu pressed his thumbs deeper into the knot below the man's left scapula, coaxing it loose, the way he had coaxed loose ten thousand such knots across the years.

"There," Liu said in his careful English, and patted the man twice. "Finished. You carry your worry here, in the shoulders. Tell your wife to remind you to drop them."

The German laughed, a big easy sound, and pressed a folded bill into Liu's palm without counting it. Liu folded it again by feel — the size told him it was a hundred, generous — and tucked it into the breast pocket of his tunic. He bowed his head. He did not need eyes to know the man was already walking away, already pulling out a phone, already half a city block gone in his mind.

That was the thing people never understood about the blind. They thought darkness was the absence of the world. For Liu it was the opposite. The world pressed in on him constantly, through his fingers and his ears and the soles of his feet, through the smell of frying dough and diesel and wet stone, through the temperature of a stranger's skin and the rhythm of a stranger's breath. He had been a sighted boy once, until he was ten, until the day the scaffolding came down on the building site where his father worked and a length of falling pipe took the light out of the world forever. Fifty years he had lived in the dark since then. He no longer remembered, truly, what green looked like, or his mother's face. But he remembered the shape of things. He had built a whole second world out of shape and sound, and he lived in it the way other men lived in apartments, comfortably, knowing where every wall stood.

"Going to pour all night," said Wen, the woman at the table beside his. She was younger than Liu, in her forties, blind from birth, and she had a laugh like a struck bell. "You should call Lee. Tell him to come early."

"Lee comes when he comes," Liu said. He began to wipe down his table with the damp cloth, working by touch along the vinyl, folding the thin blanket into its precise thirds. The parlor was a row of such tables under a long awning, set into a covered arcade off the main pedestrian street — a place the city had built, or said it had built, for the welfare of its blind citizens. *A model of socialist compassion*, the plaque at the entrance read; Wen had told him so, laughing, the first week he'd worked here. The truth was simpler and less flattering. The blind worked cheap. The tourists liked the novelty. And the district made its cut. But it was steady, and it was honest labor, and Liu had long ago stopped expecting the world to be kinder than it was.

He found his cane where it leaned against the support pole, the grip worn smooth and dark by his hand over the years, and he sat back down on his stool to wait. The rain thickened. He could hear it now as a solid thing, a roar on the awning overhead, hissing on the stone of the empty street where an hour ago there had been the shuffle of a thousand feet. The tourists had fled to the shopping malls. The sellers had packed up. One by one his colleagues called their rides or unfolded cheap umbrellas and went out into it, calling goodnights, and the parlor emptied around him until it was only Liu and the old security guard dozing in his plastic chair by the gate.

Liu took out his phone and pressed the worn button that made it speak. *Seven forty-one,* the tinny voice told him. *No new messages.*

Lee was late.

It was not like his son. Lee was a careful boy — a man now, thirty-two, with a wife and a job at a logistics firm out past the Fifth Ring, and a face Liu had never seen as an adult, only mapped with his fingertips on holidays, the strong jaw, the soft mouth he'd gotten from his mother. Every evening Lee drove in from his shift to collect his father, and every evening they sat in the little car in the choking traffic on the long road home to Pinggu, and they talked. It was the best part of Liu's day. Lee would tell him about the trucks and the shipping manifests and his wife's cooking, and Liu would tell him which tourists had tipped well and which had tried to haggle, and for forty minutes in the dark behind the windshield they were simply father and son, the way they had been when Lee was small and the world was simpler.

But tonight there was no Lee.

Liu pressed the button again. *Seven fifty-three. No new messages.*

The irritation rose in him before he could stop it. He knew it for the old fault it was — his temper, quick as a struck match, the thing his late wife had teased him for and his mother had despaired of. *You are too proud, Xiao,* his mother used to say. *Pride is a cane that breaks when you lean on it.* But he leaned on it anyway. He had always leaned on it. Pride was what had carried him off the floor of the blind school where the other children had wept for their mothers; pride was what had put a trade in his hands and food on his family's table; pride was the reason that now, at sixty years old, soaked through with the damp that crept under the awning, Liu Xiao made the decision that would end one life and begin another.

He stood. He shook out the cheap plastic poncho he kept folded in his bag and pulled it over his head. He found the security guard with a few words and told him he was leaving, and the old man mumbled that he was a fool to walk in this, and Liu said he had walked in worse, which was a lie, and tapped his way out from under the awning into the rain.

It hit him like a wall.

For a moment he simply stood, the water sheeting off the poncho, drumming on his scalp through the hood, and he nearly turned back. He knew the route from here to within a few blocks of home — he had counted it out in his head a hundred times, the turn at the bank with the marble steps, the long straightaway past the noodle shops, the smell of the canal where the road bent south. He had never walked the whole of it alone. But he was a self-sufficient man. He had told the President of his own household, his dead father, that he would never be a burden, and he had kept that vow for fifty years. He would not break it now standing in the rain like a child waiting to be fetched.

So Liu Xiao set out.

The cane found the curb, the gutter running full and cold over the tip of it, and he stepped down into it and crossed when the sound of the traffic told him to cross. The rain changed everything. It was his enemy and his blindfold both — it flattened the world, drowned out the small sounds he steered by, turned the pavement into a slick uncertain country under his feet. He went slowly. He counted his steps. He kept his right shoulder toward the buildings, brushing the wet stone now and then with the back of his hand to keep his line, and the cane swept and tapped, swept and tapped, the only steady thing in a roaring world.

He found the bank by its marble steps, just where it should be. He found the noodle shops by their smell, the broth and the scallion oil cutting through the rain. Pride sang in him, fierce and warm. *You see,* he told the rain. *You see, old man. You can still do it.*

It took him the better part of an hour to come within a few blocks of his own building. He knew he was close. He could smell the canal, the green muddy breath of it, and somewhere ahead and to the left a dog was barking the same three notes over and over the way the dog by the bridge always did. And then, cutting across the rain, he heard the shouting.

It came from up ahead, across the street — several voices, men's voices, raised and ugly in the way that meant a fight or the moments before one. Liu stopped. His old instincts, the survivor's instincts of a blind man in a city that did not forgive carelessness, told him to turn, to go around, to put a wall between himself and trouble he could not see and could not run from. He had half-turned to do it.

Then someone shouted his name.

"*Liu Xiao!*"

He froze. The voice was wrong — not Lee's, not anyone's he knew, and yet it knew him, it called him by both his names across the dark and the rain as though it had been waiting for him, as though this whole night, the late son and the lonely walk and the rain itself, had been a current drawing him to exactly this corner at exactly this moment. A cold that had nothing to do with the water went through him.

He did not turn around.

Instead, the weight came.

It fell on him all at once, from everywhere — as if the air had become water and the water had become stone. His lungs crushed flat in his chest. He could not draw breath to cry out. He felt himself being stretched and squeezed in the same instant, pulled long like wire and pressed small like clay, every part of him at once, a pressure so total and so strange that pain was too small a word for it. The cane fell from his hand. He did not hear it hit the ground. There was a sound in his skull like the whole sky tearing along a seam, and then a light — an impossible thing, a *light*, the first light Liu Xiao had perceived in fifty years, blooming white and enormous behind eyes that had forgotten how to see —

— and then nothing at all.

---

He woke on his back, on concrete, with the rain gone.

For a long, drifting moment Liu lay still and could not understand what was wrong, because nothing hurt, and that was wrong; his knees always hurt when he lay on a hard floor, his hip always complained. He felt rested. He felt, strangely, *young* — the way he had felt at twenty, loose and easy in his own body. And there was light. He had been so long in the dark that at first his mind refused the word for it, groping after some other explanation: warmth, perhaps, the sun on his face. But it was not warmth. It was light, and it was *seen*, taken in through his open eyes and carried somewhere behind them that had been a shuttered empty room for fifty years and was now, suddenly, blazing.

Above him stood his apartment building.

He knew it the way you know a thing in a dream and only after question how you could possibly know it — the gray concrete flank of it climbing into a pale washed sky, the rust streaks under the windows, the drying laundry hanging from a dozen balconies, a red shirt, a child's yellow trousers, a white sheet lifting in the wind. *Color.* He had no words ready for it. The yellow of the trousers went into him like a sound, like a high clear note, and he understood that this was yellow because some part of his ten-year-old self had kept it all this time, folded away, waiting.

Liu Xiao began to laugh.

He lay on the wet concrete of the courtyard of his own building, sixty years old, and he laughed up at the sun until the tears ran sideways into his ears, because he could *see*, because the world had come back to him whole and impossible and bright, and he did not care how or why, he did not care about the strangeness of waking here, blocks from where he'd fallen, with no memory of the road between. Let it be a miracle. He would take it. He had earned a miracle, surely, after fifty years.

It was only when the laughter quieted that he became aware of the murmuring.

People were gathering. He turned his head — and oh, the simple wonder of *turning his head and seeing the world swing past* — and there were faces ringing him, a dozen of them, his neighbors, strangers, a woman with a phone held up, a man crouching with concern, all of them looking not quite at him but slightly above him, at something in the air over his body. Their faces were doing something he had to learn all over again to read. Fear. They were afraid.

Liu turned his head the other way to see what they saw.

There were five of them.

Spheres — metal, or something like metal, dull and gray and seamless, each the size of a basketball — and they hung in the air around him without strings or sound, hovering, turning slowly on no axis he could name. They had no faces and no eyes and yet he felt, with a certainty that bypassed all reason and went straight to the oldest part of him, that they were *looking* at him. That they were waiting. That they belonged to him, and he to them, and that whatever they were they had been with him a long time already, longer than the few minutes since he woke, and they were glad — he could feel their gladness, a soft warm hum of it at the very back of his skull — that he was awake at last.

One of the spheres drifted closer, lowering itself toward his face the way a curious cat lowers its head. Liu, who an hour ago had been an old blind man tapping home alone in the rain, looked up into the blank gray face of the thing that had remade him, and felt no fear at all.

"Hello," he said softly, in the language of his mother. "What are you?"

The sphere hummed, and the sound of it was almost, almost like an answer.

A voice broke in from the ring of frightened faces — a young man, crouching now at the edge of Liu's vision, his hand half-raised. "*Lao xiansheng*— old sir — are you all right? Do you need help?"

And Liu Xiao, still drunk on the sight of the sky, not yet understanding anything of what he had become or what it would cost, turned toward the kind voice and the kind face and said the simplest, most human thing in the world.

"Yes," he said. "Yes — please. Help me."

The spheres heard him. And the spheres, who loved him, who had felt his distress as their own, moved to give him what he asked.

A tentacle uncoiled from the nearest sphere — long, impossibly long, nine feet of it unspooling in a heartbeat — and struck the kind young man in the back of the neck.

The screaming began.

## Chapter Two — What the Spheres Gave

For three or four seconds, the young man convulsed.

It was a terrible thing to watch, and Liu — who had spent fifty years unable to watch anything — watched it with his new eyes and felt the horror of it land in him fresh and unsoftened. The man's whole body went rigid, then shuddered, his heels drumming the concrete, his jaw clenched white, the tentacle buried in the back of his neck pulsing like a throat swallowing. The crowd recoiled as one animal. Somewhere a woman shrieked. Liu scrambled up onto his elbows — *up, so easily, no pain* — and reached out a useless hand and cried, "Stop! Let him go, *let him go*—"

And the spheres, hearing the change in him, the panic where a moment ago there had been a request for help, hesitated. A second tentacle, already half-extended toward an older man at the crowd's edge, paused in the air, quivering, uncertain.

Then it struck the second man too.

Both of them dropped to their knees. The convulsions passed as quickly as they'd come. The tentacles held them a moment longer, gently now, almost tenderly, the way a mother holds a child's head, and then both spheres seemed to come to some private decision and the tentacles withdrew — not all the way, Liu saw, but enough that the men sagged forward, gasping, alive, blinking.

The young man was the first to lift his head. And when he did, Liu Xiao saw the most extraordinary thing yet on a morning already full of impossible things.

The young man's eyes had changed. They moved across the scene — across the spheres, across Liu, across the crowd, across the cracked concrete and the laundry overhead and the puddles still bright with last night's rain — and they moved the way Liu imagined his own must have moved in the last few minutes, with a hunger, a *fluency*, as though they were reading the world like text. The man's lips parted. He looked at Liu, and a strange focused calm came over his face, the calm of a doctor stepping into a room.

"You have an implant," the young man said. His voice was wondering, distant, the voice of someone reading aloud from a page only he could see. "In your cerebral cortex. Threaded through the — through the visual pathways, and down into the brainstem. It's not metal. It's not anything. It's *grown.*" He swallowed. "How do I know that? I shouldn't know that. I drive a delivery scooter. I failed biology."

"And it is talking to those," said the second man, the older one, in a hollow voice. He had risen unsteadily and laid one trembling hand flat against the nearest sphere, and he was staring at his own palm where it touched the gray surface. "I can feel — I can read it. Like a recipe. Like a list of every — " He pulled his hand back as though burned. "It's alive. Part of it is alive, tissue, real living tissue, grown the way you grow a pearl, around — around something else. Metal that isn't metal. A machine that is also a *body.* Layered. Folded a thousand times." He looked up, wild-eyed, at the crowd. "I can taste what things are made of. I touched the railing, and I knew the iron and the rust and the old paint and the lead in the old paint. What is *happening* to me?"

Liu understood, then — not the how of it, but the shape of it. The spheres had heard him ask for help, and they had reached into two ordinary men and woken something in them, gifts chosen the way a frightened child chooses a gift for a sick parent, clumsily, lovingly, *usefully*: one to see what was wrong with him, one to know what the spheres themselves were made of. They had given the young man the knowledge of a physician. They had given the older man the sight to read the world down to its atoms. And they had done it because they loved Liu and wanted him to understand, and they did not know — they could not know — that you cannot pour a surgeon's whole knowledge into a delivery driver's skull without it feeling like being torn open.

"They are trying to help me," Liu said, and he did not know if he was explaining it to the men or to the crowd or to himself. "They felt me ask. They — they don't understand. They are children." He pressed his hand to his own chest, over his hammering heart, and beneath the fear he felt again that warm hum at the back of his skull, anxious now, *sorry,* a wordless animal apology. They had not meant to frighten anyone. They had only wanted to give him eyes that could examine him and a mind that could name them. "It's all right," Liu murmured, and he did not know to whom. "It's all right. You did well. You did well."

The young man — the doctor now, for as long as the sphere held him — had crawled to Liu's side and was running his hands over Liu with a brisk professional certainty wildly at odds with the terror in his eyes. He checked Liu's pulse, his pupils, the back of his neck.

"Your heart is sixty-two and strong," the young man reported. "Better than strong. Old sir, forgive me, but your body — there's scarring everywhere, old, the body of a man who has worked hard and aged hard, and yet underneath it everything is — *clean.* Repaired. Your liver. Your lungs. I'd swear you've smoked forty years and there's not a mark on them. Something is healing you. Faster than healing should go." He sat back on his heels. "And your eyes. Old sir. There is nothing wrong with your eyes. There hasn't been for some time, I think. The fault was always farther back, in the brain, in the place that makes seeing *mean* something — and that place is lit up like a city now. They didn't fix your eyes. They built you a new way to see, and ran it through the implant, and—"

"Stop," Liu said gently, because the young man's voice had begun to shake itself apart. "Stop. Breathe. You are not a doctor, you are a frightened young man who was kind to a stranger in a courtyard, and I am sorry for what they did to you, and I am grateful. Both at once. Breathe."

The crowd had grown. Phones bristled at every angle, a hedge of bright lenses, and Liu — who had never in his life been *looked at*, who had moved through Beijing for sixty years as a man people's eyes slid off of, an old blind nobody with a cane — felt the new and uncomfortable weight of being *seen.* The murmur had a current in it now, an electricity, the particular sound a crowd makes when it has decided that what it is witnessing is too large to keep to itself. *Did you see — the tentacles — call someone — is it a weapon — is it a god — film it, film all of it—*

And then the pressing-in began, the way crowds press, not from malice but from the simple physics of a hundred curious bodies, and the front rank stumbled forward, and the spheres — Liu felt their alarm spike, felt it in his own gut like a swallowed cold stone — drew in tight around him, their tentacles lifting, weaving, beginning to *bristle.*

The young man with the doctor's mind read the danger in the same instant Liu felt it.

"We have to get him out of here," he said. "Now. Before they hurt someone." He looked down at himself, at his own hands, and a wild incredulous expression crossed his face — and then, as simply as a man deciding to stand, he lifted off the ground.

He rose a foot, two feet, his arms windmilling once before he steadied, hanging in the air over the gasping crowd with an expression that hovered between terror and rapture. The sphere bonded to him pulsed, pleased. *That too,* Liu understood. *They gave him that too — so he could carry me.* Of course they had. They thought of everything, the spheres, in their strange lopsided way; they had given the young man a healer's knowledge to mend Liu and a flyer's body to bear Liu away from harm, all at once, all from a single touch, because Liu had asked for help and the spheres did not do things by halves.

"Old sir," the flying man said, reaching down. "Will you let me carry you? I don't — I don't know how long I'll be able to. I can feel it already, like a tide. It wants to go back. But while I can—"

Liu looked at the offered hand. He looked at the crowd, the lenses, the fear and the hunger in all those staring faces. He looked at the five spheres hanging close and tense around him, his impossible new children, humming their anxious love.

"Yes," he said again — and this time he watched the consequences of the word, watched it land, and chose it anyway. "Take me home."

The flying man caught him under the arms, and the world dropped away.

---

They did not go far. The flying man was clumsy and frightened and the power, as he'd said, was already ebbing — Liu felt it through the bond, felt the sphere's grip on the man loosening like a hand growing tired — and so they rose only as high as the rooftops and skimmed two streets over and came down, hard, in the small concrete service yard behind Liu's own building, where the bins were and where the old men played cards in summer. The five spheres poured after them through the air like a string of beads.

The flying man set Liu on his feet and immediately staggered, all the borrowed grace going out of him at once. He pressed his back against the wall, breathing hard. "I can feel it leaving," he said. "All of it. The knowing and the flying both. It's like — like trying to hold water." He laughed, a thin shaky sound. "An hour ago I was thinking about whether I could afford to fix my scooter."

For a moment Liu studied the two men with his new eyes — the young one gray and shaking against the wall, the older one sagging by the bins — and a grief he had no right to feel moved through him. They had been ordinary an hour ago. One had been worrying about a scooter; the other had simply stopped to ask an old man on the ground if he needed help. And for that small kindness the spheres had reached into them and rearranged them and would now, any moment, leave them hollowed out and marked, remembered forever as the men in the famous courtyard video, hunted by whatever the state was already sending. Liu had not asked for this power, but he had spoken the word that loosed it, and the cost of that word had been paid by strangers. It was the first time he understood, in his gut and not just his mind, that what had been given to him could not be given without taking from someone else. He would spend the rest of his life learning the full weight of that lesson. It began here, in a service yard, with two frightened men he had ruined by accident.

"Go," Liu told them. "Both of you. Go home. Tell no one your names, if you can help it. They will come looking, and you do not want to be found. You were strangers who helped an old man. Nothing more."

The older man, the one who could read the composition of things, had stumbled after them down an alley and now sagged against the bins, gray-faced, the knowledge draining out of him too. He nodded mutely. He looked, Liu thought, as if he had been crying.

"What are you?" the flying man asked. It was the same question Liu had asked the sphere, and Liu had no better answer than the sphere had given him.

"I don't know," Liu said. "An hour ago I was a blind man." He almost smiled. "Now I am something else. We will both have to find out what."

The man held his gaze a moment longer — and then the last of the sphere's gift left him, Liu felt the tentacle slip free of the bond entirely and reel back into the sphere, and the man's eyes were just a frightened young man's eyes again, ordinary, exhausted. He turned and ran. The older man pushed himself off the bins and followed, lurching, and then Liu Xiao stood alone in the service yard of his own building with five humming spheres and a sky he could see.

He looked up at the building. Five floors. His apartment on the fourth, the window with the cracked pane Lee kept promising to fix. *Lee.* Where was Lee? The whole night came back to him — the rain, the late son, the voice that had called his name — and a new fear, an ordinary human fear, cut clean through all the wonder. His family did not know. They thought him lost in the rain. He had to get upstairs. He had to call Lee, hear his son's voice, tell him — tell him *what?*

Liu started toward the stairwell. The spheres fell in behind him, drifting in a loose obedient line, humming, and as he climbed the dim concrete stairs they followed him up and around each landing like five patient puppies trailing their master home.

He had made it to the second landing when he heard the voices above him, in the corridor outside his own door — flat official voices, the crackle of a radio, the particular timbre of men in uniform who have been told to wait.

The police had gotten there first.

Liu Xiao stopped on the stairs, his hand on the cold rail, his new eyes turned up toward the light spilling down from the floor above, and behind him the five spheres slowed and hung in the stairwell, humming softly, waiting on his will.

## Chapter Three — A Loyal Citizen

There were four officers in the corridor, and they stopped talking when Liu Xiao stepped off the stairwell into the light.

He saw their faces — saw, for the first time in his life, the precise way fear and bewilderment moved across living human features, the widening, the slackening, the unconscious half-step backward. The youngest of them, a boy really, had his hand on his sidearm and didn't seem to know it. The oldest, a stocky sergeant with a tired face and gray at his temples, simply stared past Liu at the five spheres that came drifting up out of the stairwell behind him, one after another, to hang humming in the corridor over the old man's shoulders.

For a long moment no one spoke. The fluorescent tube overhead buzzed. From behind one of the doors came the sound of a television, a game show, canned laughter, absurd and ordinary.

"Liu Xiao?" the sergeant said at last. His voice was careful. "Resident, apartment 4-0-3?"

"Yes," Liu said. He kept his hands loose and visible at his sides, the way he had learned long ago to do around nervous men, the way a blind man learns to telegraph his harmlessness. "I am Liu Xiao. This is my home. My family is inside — please, are they all right? Is my son here?"

"There's no one inside," the sergeant said. "We knocked. Sir, I'm going to need you to — to keep those *things* still. Can you do that?"

"They are still," Liu said gently. "They will not hurt you. They only follow me." And to prove it, or to reassure himself, he sent a thought toward them, wordless, a feeling of *calm, be calm, these are friends* — and felt the answering hum soften, and watched the youngest officer flinch as the nearest sphere settled lower and went quiet, like a dog lying down. "I don't fully understand them myself, officer. I will tell you everything I know. I have done nothing wrong. I am a loyal citizen. I love my country. I will go with you, and I will answer every question, and I ask only that you tell me my family is safe."

The honesty of it seemed to reach the sergeant. Something in the man's shoulders came down a fraction. He was, Liu sensed, a decent person at the end of a long shift who had been sent to a routine call — *neighbor reports a man carried off by a flying figure, possibly drunk, possibly assault* — and had instead found himself in a concrete corridor facing five floating impossibilities and a calm old man who claimed to be their master. There was no procedure for this. There was no procedure for any of this. The sergeant was improvising, the way Liu was improvising, the way the whole world would soon be improvising around the fact of him.

"Your family's not here, but there's no sign anything's wrong," the sergeant said. "We'll find out where they are. Right now I need you to come with us. You understand we have to — we have to report this. I don't even know what to call it. I don't know what *box to tick.*" He let out a breath that was almost a laugh, helpless. "Will those things follow you into the car?"

"They go where I go," Liu said. "I am sorry. I cannot send them away. I have tried, since I woke. They will not leave me." He paused. "But they are gentle. I give you my word, sergeant, on the memory of my mother — they are gentle, unless someone tries to hurt me. Do not let your men try to hurt me, and no one will be harmed."

The sergeant looked at the young officer with his hand on his gun, and said, quietly, "Wei. Off the weapon." And the boy snatched his hand away as though the grip had grown hot.

They went down together — Liu and four policemen and five spheres — and out into the courtyard where, a lifetime ago, he had woken laughing under the sun. A small crowd had reformed at the edges, kept back now by two more officers and a line of plastic tape, and they raised their phones as Liu emerged, and a sound went through them, a collective indrawn breath, and Liu Xiao walked out into the eyes of the world for the second time that day. He did not yet know that some of those phones were already feeding the images out across the country, that within the hour the clip of the old blind man and his floating spheres would be flickering across ten million screens before the censors woke to it, that he was already, before he had even reached the police car, becoming something the state would have to reckon with. He knew only that he was tired, and that his family was somewhere out in the city not knowing whether he lived, and that the sky, which he could see, had cleared to a soft washed gray after the rain.

They put him in the back of a car. The spheres would not fit, and there followed an absurd, almost tender negotiation — the sergeant standing in the courtyard with his hands spread, saying *they cannot fly through the city behind a moving vehicle, it will cause panic, it will cause accidents* — until Liu, leaning out the open door, called the spheres to him and they came and pressed themselves against the car, two on the roof, two at the windows, one across the hood, and rode like that, clinging, humming, all the way to the station, a sight that stopped traffic on three ring roads and made the evening broadcasts in four provinces before midnight.

---

They questioned him for a long time.

It was not unkind, the questioning, not at first. They gave him tea. They asked him his name and his work and his family and where he had been, and Liu told them the truth, all of it, the late son and the rain and the walk and the voice that called his name and the weight that crushed him and the waking under the sun. He told them about the two men in the courtyard and what the spheres had done to them, and he begged the officers, when they came looking, to be gentle with those two — *they were only kind to me, they did nothing, the spheres chose them, not the other way* — and he believed, watching the sergeant write it all down, that he had been heard.

But the report, once filed, did not stay in the small precinct. Liu watched it leave. He watched the sergeant take a phone call and go pale and stand up straighter; he watched men he had not seen before arrive in dark coats and speak in low voices and look at him through the glass; he watched, over the course of that first long night, the matter rise up out of the hands of tired beat policemen and into the hands of others, and others above them, until by morning there were men in the room with him whose authority Liu could feel the way he had once felt the temperature of a stranger's skin — a cold competent weight, the weight of the state itself turning its full attention upon one old man and five humming spheres.

It was strange, that long first night of questions, to sit in the bright station and read the faces of the men who questioned him. Liu had never in his life watched a man decide whether to believe him. He had heard it — heard the hesitations, the shifts in breath, the careful flatness people put into their voices when they doubted him — but he had never *seen* it, the flicker at the corner of an eye, the glance two officers traded when they thought the blind man could not catch them at it. He caught all of it now. It was like being given a second language at sixty. And what it told him, again and again across that night, was that they were afraid of him, and that their fear was the kind that hardens into policy. A man they could not explain was a man they could not control, and a man they could not control was, to the machinery slowly waking around him, a problem to be managed rather than a person to be helped. He had loved his country his whole life. He understood, that night, watching the dark coats arrive, that his country did not quite know how to love him back — only how to keep him.

They moved him before dawn. Not to a cell — they were careful, in those early hours, not to call anything a cell, not to use any word that might frighten him or anger the things that floated at his shoulders. *A facility. For your safety and ours. For study. You'll be comfortable.* And he was comfortable, in a clean bare suite of rooms somewhere beneath or beyond the ordinary city, with a bed and a chair and food brought on trays and no windows at all, which did not trouble Liu the way it would have troubled a sighted man, because he had lived without windows his whole life and learned to find the world by other means. The spheres came with him. They would not have let themselves be parted from him, and the men in the dark coats, watching the readouts on their machines, had quickly understood that any attempt to part the spheres from Liu Xiao produced in them an agitation that flickered, on every instrument, like the first tremor of an earthquake.

So they kept them together, the old man and his spheres, and they studied them.

For three weeks — Liu counted the days by the meals — the scientists came. He liked some of them. There was a woman, Dr. Han, sharp and patient, who treated Liu as a colleague in a shared mystery rather than a specimen, who explained to him what they were learning even when she was surely not supposed to. It was from Dr. Han that Liu came to understand his own miracle from the outside.

The spheres were alien, she told him — truly alien, not of this earth, a thing the instruments confirmed a hundred ways. They were psionically bonded to him through the implant grown into his cortex, the *Mother Muo* as she had taken to calling the seed of tissue at the heart of his brain; through it he could summon them, direct them, feel their feelings, all without a word or a gesture. They were a fusion, just as the man in the courtyard had said, of grown tissue and engineered structure, alive and machine at once. They did not eat or drink. They drew their power from some source the machines could not name, and they rested when Liu slept, going dim and quiet through the long nights as though they dreamed.

When they touched a person, they could grant a power — temporary, random, drawn from some vast unknowable menu, the way they had granted flight and medicine and atomic sight in the courtyard. When they let go, the power vanished. They could lift, study, examine; their long tentacles drifted out as they hovered and brushed against anything alive or anything dangerous and *learned* it, the way a blind man's hand learns a face. They were, Dr. Han said, the most extraordinary thing humanity had ever encountered, and she said it with wonder, and also — Liu heard this too, under the wonder — with fear.

Because they had also found what the spheres were afraid of.

"Nanites," Dr. Han told him one evening, sitting across from him in the bare bright room, her voice low though they both knew the room was listening. "Engineered machines, smaller than a cell. We brought a sealed sample near them, for the tests. Liu — I have never seen anything react the way they reacted." Her hands were not quite steady around her cup. "They panicked. All five at once. The readings went off every scale we had. If I hadn't ordered the sample sealed away again, I think — I think they would have done something terrible to get away from it." She looked at him. "And there's more. We ran the models. If a Muo sphere were exposed to nanites long enough, it would die. And when it died — Liu, when one of them *dies* — it releases something. A nanovirus. Engineered, contagious, and it does not kill cleanly. It eats the human brain. It leaves the body walking, enraged, mindless. Hateful. We modeled a single dying sphere in a closed city. The number it gave us—" She stopped. She did not finish.

Liu sat very still. He could feel the five spheres around him, dim in their evening rest, dreaming whatever the spheres dreamed, innocent of the doom Dr. Han had just described, the gentle terrible thing they carried at the heart of themselves. He thought of his family, still out there in the city, whom he had been allowed to call exactly once, his son's voice cracking on the line — *Baba, they say you're safe, they say you're helping them, is it true, is it true* — and he had said yes, it was true, he was helping, and they would be looked after, and Lee should not worry, and Lee should not come looking.

"Then we must keep them far from such things," Liu said quietly. "Forever. That is all."

"That's not how the men upstairs are thinking about it," Dr. Han said, and there was a warning in it, and a sadness. She rose to go. At the door she paused, and without turning back she said, "They've made a decision about you. About what you'll be. The President wants to meet you, Liu. Tomorrow." A breath. "Be careful what you agree to. Whatever they offer — be careful. You're not a man to them anymore. You're a weapon they haven't decided how to aim."

The door closed. The lock turned, soft and final.

Liu Xiao sat alone in the bright bare room with his sleeping spheres, sixty years old, three weeks sighted, and understood that the dark he had lived in for fifty years had only ever been the simpler of the two kinds.

## Chapter Four — The Protector of China

They dressed him for the dinner.

Liu had never owned a tunic as fine as the one they gave him — deep slate-gray silk, high-collared, the buttons worked in a darker thread, the kind of garment a scholar might have worn in another century. They cut his hair and combed it back and bound it in the neat queue he had always favored, and a quiet young man held a mirror up so that Liu could see himself, and Liu looked, and for a long moment did not recognize the old man looking back. He had never seen his own adult face. He found it strange and grave and lined and not unkind, the face of a man who had carried things. The spheres hovered behind his reflection, five gray moons, and Liu thought: *Whoever that is, it is not the man who walked home in the rain.*

The Presidential Palace, when they brought him to it, was nothing he could have imagined and everything he could have feared — vast quiet halls of red and gold, lacquered screens, the smell of old wood and beeswax and, underneath, the faint cold smell of power, which Liu had decided was the smell of rooms where the air is too still because no one in them breathes easily. He was led through corridors hung with calligraphy and into a small private dining room where a single round table had been set for two, and there, rising to greet him with a politician's perfect warmth, was the President of China.

"Liu Xiao," the President said, and took both of Liu's hands in his, and his hands were warm and dry and strong. "The man of the hour. The man the whole world is whispering about. Sit, sit. You must be tired of bare rooms and men in white coats. Tonight, no coats. Tonight we are just two sons of China sharing a meal."

He was younger than Liu had expected, the President — or younger-seeming, broad-faced and composed, with eyes that did not change when his mouth smiled. He had a way of looking at the spheres, Liu noticed, as Liu took his seat and the spheres arranged themselves at his back: a single sweeping glance, appraising, that took their measure the way a buyer takes the measure of a horse, and then moved on as though they were of no account at all. It was a controlled glance. It was meant to be controlled. Liu, who read men through their breath and the small betrayals of their bodies, heard the President's heartbeat tick faster as the spheres settled, and slow again deliberately, and understood that the man was afraid, and had decided long ago never to let his fear be seen.

They ate. The food was extraordinary — Liu had spent his life eating with his fingers what he could not see, and now he could see it, the lacquer-red of the duck skin, the jade of the greens, the way steam curled off the rice — and the President was a generous and charming host, and they spoke of small things, of Beijing, of Liu's work, of the strange roads life took.

"You worked the parlor on Wangfujing," the President said, refilling Liu's cup himself, a small staged intimacy Liu noted and let pass. "I am told you were the most respected man there. That tourists asked for you by name." He smiled. "There is a kind of poetry in it, don't you think? The state built that parlor for its blind, and out of it came the one man in China who can see what no one else can. You were always meant for more than that table, Liu."

"I was content at that table," Liu said. He did not say it sharply, but he did not let it go either. He had learned, in three weeks of bare rooms, that men like this one collected your softness and spent it later, and he meant to give this one as little as he could. "I had work I was good at, and people who respected me, and a son who came each evening to drive me home. That is more than most men get, Mr. President. I would trade all of this to have it back, if the spheres would let me. They will not. So here we are."

A flicker, again, behind the President's eyes — that brief recalculation, the noting of a man who could not be flattered into forgetting what he had lost. But the President only inclined his head, as though Liu had said something gracious, and steered them on. He was good at it, Liu thought, this steering. He had steered a billion and a half people. One old blind man was nothing to him.

And then, somewhere between the fish and the soup, the President set down his chopsticks, and the air in the still room grew stiller.

"So, Liu," he said. "Tell me about these Muo spheres."

And Liu told him — what they were, alien and bonded, summoned without word or gesture; how they attached and detached with their tentacles, lifted and touched and studied; how they had not learned to speak and only hummed as they drifted; how, when they touched a person, they granted a temporary, random power, and how, when they let go, the power vanished like a dream on waking. He told it plainly, the way Dr. Han had taught him to tell it, and the President listened with his unchanging eyes.

"And you have all of these abilities yourself?"

"I'm not sure," Liu said. "My eyesight has improved enough for me to see. Otherwise I feel exactly as I did twenty years ago." He did not mention the healing — that he had smoked a cigarette one of the guards had given him, the first in three weeks, and felt nothing of the old morning cough; that the scientists' machines said his body was mending itself faster than any old man's body should. He kept that to himself. A man should keep something to himself, in a room like this.

"How did you come to possess them?"

"I don't know. I was walking home one night, and they appeared to me."

"Do you know their purpose?"

Liu was quiet a moment. He reached, in himself, toward the warm humming presence of the spheres, and felt their gentle attention turn toward his question as though they too were curious. "I'm not certain," he said slowly. "But I think they are children of their kind. And I think they see me as one of their own."

The President considered this. "And that," he said, "is why you accepted our offer to become Protector of China?"

For the offer had been made — not here, not yet in words, but Liu understood it had been hanging over him since the moment Dr. Han said *they've made a decision about what you'll be.* He had had three weeks in a bare room to think about what he would say. He thought of his son's cracking voice on the telephone. He thought of his daughter-in-law, of the small shoddy apartment in Pinggu, of all the ordinary people he had passed his life among, blind and overlooked, whom the state could lift up or grind down at a whim. He thought of Dr. Han's warning — *you're a weapon they haven't decided how to aim* — and he thought that a weapon with no will of its own is aimed by others, but a weapon that agrees, that bargains, that keeps its dignity and names its price, retains at least the ghost of a choice.

"Yes," Liu said.

"Incredible." The President leaned back. "We live in a dangerous age, Liu. You must know this now, after three weeks with my scientists. The world is changing. Men and women are appearing — abducted as you were, or altered, or mutated, or built — with powers that armies cannot answer. The Americans have them. The Russians. There is a man in Tanzania, they say, who is a living weapon worth a division. China cannot be defenseless in such a world. China will not be." His eyes found Liu's and held them. "We must find a way to harness this power. To defend China against the new superweapon threats. With you at our side, Liu Xiao — with the Protector of China standing on the wall — we need fear no one."

It was a beautiful speech, and it had been made many times, Liu thought, to many men, in many rooms like this. He let it settle. And then he said the thing he had decided, in three weeks of darkness, that he would not leave unsaid.

"I would be happy to help, Mr. President. But they will not like it."

"How do you know?"

"I can sense their emotions," Liu said. "They are not happy when they are used as weapons. They are innocent beings. They don't understand war, or violence. They only want to protect me, and to help others. If you ask me to make them into a sword, I will feel them flinch from it every time. I want you to know that before we begin. I will not pretend it away to make this easier."

Something flickered behind the President's eyes — annoyance, perhaps, or calculation, or the simple irritation of a man told *no* in a room where no one tells him *no.* But he mastered it, as he mastered everything, and when he spoke his voice was warm and reasonable and absolutely immovable.

"I understand your concern, Liu. Truly. But we must think of the greater good — the safety of our country, of a billion and a half souls who depend on the wall holding. We will find a way to use their abilities without harming them. You have my word."

Liu set down his own chopsticks. He let the silence stretch, the way he had learned to let a knot under his hands resist before it gave. He could feel the spheres behind him, stirred by the heat in the conversation, their tentacles drifting, restless; he sent them a wordless calm and felt them settle, and saw the President watch that small private exchange with a stillness that was its own kind of fear.

"You should understand what you are asking me to do," Liu said quietly. "When I send them against a man, I do not feel triumph, Mr. President. I feel them recoil. It is like making a child strike another child — they will do it, because they love me, because they would do anything I asked. That is exactly why I must be careful what I ask. A thing that will refuse you nothing must be commanded gently, or you ruin it." He paused. "I have ruined things in my life by handling them roughly. My temper has cost me. I am an old man, and I know my own faults. I am telling you mine so that you will believe me when I name the danger in yours."

"And what is my fault, Liu?" the President asked. There was a glint of real curiosity in it now, beneath the politician's warmth — the curiosity of a man rarely spoken to so plainly.

"You see them as a resource," Liu said. "I have heard it in every word tonight. *Harness. Use. Asset.* You cannot help it; it is how you have learned to see everything, a billion and a half souls included. But they are not a resource. They are children who crossed something I cannot imagine to find me. If you treat them as a quarry to be mined, Mr. President, you will get ore for a while, and then one day the mine will close, and you will not understand why, because you never understood what it was you were taking." He met the unchanging eyes. "I will trust your judgment. But please — do not forget their well-being. They are not machines, whatever your scientists' instruments say. They feel. They are afraid of things, and they grieve, and they love. If you forget that, Mr. President, I think we will both come to regret it."

"I promise you," the President said, and smiled, and the smile did not reach his unchanging eyes. "We will do our best to protect them, too."

They returned to the meal. They spoke of practicalities — of the title, of the monitoring Liu would submit to, the watchers, the cameras, the team of handlers; of the protections that would, in exchange, be extended to Liu's family, a better apartment, a stipend, a guarantee of safety; of how Liu would be deployed, and trained, and presented to the people. Liu agreed to all of it. He traded the name his mother had given him for a title that sounded more like a job than a man, and he did it with his eyes open, both kinds of eyes, because he had decided that a cage you enter on your own terms is at least partly a fortress.

And across the table, as they ate and planned, the President of China watched the old man and his five gentle humming spheres, and behind his unchanging eyes he was already, quietly, turning over a question he would never speak aloud at this table — the question that would shadow everything that came after.

*Is this man a shield I have acquired,* the President wondered, *or a sword that has acquired me? And if the day ever comes when I cannot tell the difference — what then will I do with the Protector of China?*

## Chapter Five — The Wall Holds

Liu Xiao was exhausted.

Days — or was it weeks now — of negotiation and back-and-forth, of officials and lawyers and men whose titles he was never told, and at the end of it all he had finally signed the thing they wanted signed. In exchange for his freedom, and for advantages for his family he had not dared hope to win, he would no longer go by the name his mother had given him. From this day forward he was the Protector of China. He was not sure what to make of the title. It sounded, he had thought more than once, more like a job description than a man. But he had learned, in those rooms, when to ask questions and when to keep them behind his teeth. Mostly he was simply glad to be out — glad, after the bare windowless weeks, to be on his way home to his family.

They released him at dusk, and they made a ceremony of it.

He had not expected the crowd. He had expected to be put in a car and driven quietly to Pinggu, but instead they brought him out through the front of the great building into a plaza where thousands had been gathered — orderly, ringed by soldiers, waving small red flags — and a wall of cameras stood waiting, and a banner three stories tall hung down the building's face with his title on it in characters of gold. *护国者.* Protector of China. The state had decided, Liu understood, that he would not be a secret. A secret weapon frightens a nation. A *celebrated* weapon reassures it. So they had chosen, that first evening, to give him to the people, and the people — who had been watching the shaky phone clips for weeks, the floating spheres, the flying man, the blind massage therapist made miraculous — roared for him.

The sound went through Liu like a wave. He stood at the top of the broad steps in his slate-gray silk with the five spheres rising and turning above him, gilded now by the setting sun, and he looked out at the sea of upturned faces — faces he could *see*, thousands of them, more faces than he had seen in fifty years of darkness combined — and a swell of feeling rose in him that he could not name and did not entirely trust. Pride. Fear. A terrible tenderness. These were his people. He had spent his whole life among them unseen, an old blind nobody whose eyes the city's eyes slid off of, and now they were chanting a name that was not even a name, *Protector, Protector, Protector,* and they were chanting it at him.

*He was their protector now,* he thought, *and he would do everything in his power to keep them safe.*

He did not yet know what that would cost. He could not have known. As he raised one hand to the crowd — and the crowd's roar doubled — Liu Xiao felt, for one bright treacherous moment, almost happy.

---

The Test came three months later, on a clear cold morning, on the northern edge of Beijing.

They had trained him, in those three months, in a compound out past the mountains where no civilians came. They had taught him — or rather, they had watched while he taught himself — the reach of what he could do. He learned that he could direct the spheres with a precision that surprised even Dr. Han, splitting them, ranging them, sending one a mile out to scout and feeling through it as though it were his own far-flung hand. He learned to grant the powers deliberately, to choose a soldier from the training ranks and let a sphere touch him and watch what gift unfolded — strength, once, that let a thin conscript bend a steel bar; speed another time; once a terrible thing where a man's skin turned to something like glass and the spheres, frightened, broke the bond at once. The powers were random, always random, drawn from a well no one could see the bottom of, but Liu could feel, dimly, the spheres reaching into that well *for* the man before them, choosing as best they could, trying — always trying — to help. And every time he made them do it, he felt them flinch, just as he had told the President they would. They did not like to be reached into. They did not like to be aimed. He felt their reluctance as a softness in his own chest, and he learned to live with it, the way you learn to live with any grief.

He hated the training of them as weapons. He did it anyway. He had signed.

There were small mercies in those months, and Liu held to them. They let him see his family on the weekends — Lee would drive out to the compound gate, the same careful boy who had once collected him from the parlor in the rain, and they would sit in the visitors' room under the cameras and talk, and Liu would tell his son which of the soldiers had been kind and which had not, the way he had once told him which tourists tipped well. Lee was afraid all the time now; Liu could hear it under everything his son said, the fear of a man whose father fought monsters on the evening news. "You don't have to be a hero, Baba," Lee said once, low, glancing at the cameras. "You were enough before. You were always enough." And Liu had taken his son's hand across the table and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that the cameras would let him say, and because his son was right and it changed nothing.

It was for Lee, in the end, and for the people in the courtyard, and for the boy he had not yet met in the smoke, that Liu kept training his gentle children to do the violent thing. He told himself it was a wall he was building, and that walls, however ugly, kept the rain off the people you loved. He would learn, later, what the wall had cost. But not yet. For now he trained, and he hated it, and he did it anyway, because he had signed, and because a man who has given his word leans on it the way he leans on a cane, even when it is breaking under his hand.

The attack, when it came, did not announce itself. Liu was at the compound when the alarms went up — a contact on the northern ring road, moving fast toward the city, *superhuman, multiple, hostile,* the words coming clipped and urgent over the radios. They were mutants, the briefing said, altered men, a cell of them that had been festering somewhere in the industrial north, men whose bodies had been twisted by something — a leaked agent, a botched experiment, a power that came from rage itself — into things that were no longer quite men. There were five of them. They had already torn through a checkpoint. They were coming for the heart of the capital, for the cameras and the crowds, to make the kind of statement that such men make, and the army's ordinary weapons had slowed them and bled them and not stopped them.

They put Liu in a helicopter, and the spheres rode clinging to its skids, and they flew him to the wall.

He would remember it afterward in pieces, the way you remember the worst hours of your life. The ring road below, the abandoned cars, the smoke. The five figures advancing up the empty highway — too large, wrong-jointed, one of them dragging a ruined lamppost like a club, another leaving the asphalt cracked and steaming where its feet struck. The soldiers fallen back behind their barricades, firing, the rounds sparking off hides that should not have turned bullets. And then the helicopter touching down, and Liu Xiao stepping out into the cold and the noise, sixty years old, in his slate-gray silk, with five humming spheres at his back, the whole of China watching through a hundred lenses, the wall depending on whether one old man could hold it.

He did not feel like a hero. He felt like a frightened man who had given his word.

The mutants saw him. They knew him — everyone knew him by then — and the one with the lamppost bellowed something that was almost laughter and broke into a charge, and the road shook, and behind his barricade a young soldier was screaming into a radio, and Liu Xiao reached for his spheres.

He did not have to speak, or raise an arm. He only had to *want* it, and the wanting was enough, and the five spheres leapt from his back like loosed falcons.

What followed was not a battle the way the films would later make it. It was faster than that, and stranger, and it broke something in Liu that never fully healed. He sent two spheres low and fast to take the legs of the charging giant — their long tentacles lashing out, not to grant power now but to *bind,* to wrap and seize, nine-foot tendrils whipping around the thing's tree-trunk ankles and snapping taut, and the giant went down full-length on the highway with a crash that Liu felt through the soles of his feet. He sent a sphere to the soldiers — touched their bravest with a tentacle and gave him, in a heartbeat, a strength to match the monsters, and the young man rose up roaring with a power that was not his and would not last and hurled the steaming-footed mutant back twenty meters into a burning car. He sent a sphere to read the one whose hide turned bullets, and felt through it the composition of that armored skin, the flaw in it, the soft place — and aimed the soldiers' fire there, and the thing fell.

And he held the fifth sphere back, close to himself, because he was afraid, and because some animal wisdom in him said: *keep one. Always keep one.*

It was over in less than four minutes. Four of the mutants lay broken or bound on the highway. The fifth — the smallest, the one Liu had taken last — was not, when Liu's spheres at last pinned it shrieking to the asphalt and he walked close enough to see its face, a monster at all. It was a boy. Eighteen, perhaps. Beneath the swollen wrongness of what had been done to him there was a boy's terrified face, weeping, snarling, begging in the same breath, a child who had been altered against his will and pointed at a city and told to be a weapon, exactly — *exactly* — as Liu himself had been pointed and told.

Liu stood over him in the smoke with the cameras drinking it all in, and he felt the soldiers waiting for the order to finish it, and he felt his spheres recoiling from the violence they had been made to do, sick with it, humming a low grieving note he had never heard from them before.

"Don't kill him," Liu said.

The order came back through the radio, flat, from somewhere safe and far away: *Neutralize all hostiles.*

"He is a boy," Liu said, louder, to the soldiers, to the cameras, to the whole watching nation. "He is a boy they made into this, the same as they made *me* into this. I am the Protector of China. I am telling you. We do not kill children here." And he stood over the weeping altered boy with his five spheres ranged around them both, between the boy and the guns, and he did not move, and after a long terrible moment the guns lowered, because no soldier in that smoke wanted to be the one who fired through the Protector of China to kill a child on live television.

They took the boy alive. They took all of them who lived.

And that — more than the four-minute victory, more than the broken giants on the highway — was the image that went around China that night and around the world the morning after: the old blind man made miraculous, standing in the smoke between the guns and the weeping monster-child, his five gray spheres turning slowly above him in the cold clear light, refusing to let the wall be defended with a murder.

They called it the day the Protector saved Beijing. The state's broadcasts called it that, and the people called it that, and Liu's fame, already vast, became something else entirely after that morning — became a kind of love, fierce and national and a little frightening, the love of a billion people for a symbol they needed to be true.

Liu Xiao went home to Pinggu that night, to the better apartment they had given his family, and he held his son for a long time without speaking, and Lee — who had watched it all on the television, the guns and the monsters and his sixty-year-old father standing alone in the smoke — wept against his shoulder and said, "Baba, you could have died, you could have *died,*" and Liu, who had felt the same thing, did not say *no.* He only held his son, and over Lee's shoulder he watched his five spheres drift dim and quiet into the corners of the room to rest, grieving still, gentle beings who had been made to break men, and he wondered for the first time, plainly, whether he had made a terrible mistake.

In a quiet office in the center of the city, the President of China watched the same footage, again and again — the old man between the guns and the boy, defying a direct order on live television before the eyes of the world. He watched the people fall in love with it. And he understood, with a cold clarity, that the Protector of China had just demonstrated, in front of everyone, that there was a point past which he could not be commanded.

"Watch him," the President said quietly to the man beside him. "Closely. From now on, I want to know everything."

## Chapter Six — The Watcher and the Cough

They sent a man named Muyang Yang to watch him.

Liu noticed him within a day. By then Liu had grown used to being watched — the cameras in his apartment, the handlers who shadowed him, the soldiers at his door, the soft constant surveillance that was the price he had agreed to pay. But Muyang Yang was different. Liu felt him before he understood him: a wrongness in rooms the man had been in, a faint disturbance, as though the air there had been disarranged and smoothed back into place by something that did not quite belong to the present moment. The spheres felt it too. Their tentacles drifted toward Muyang Yang whenever he was near, brushing him, studying him, and they came away unsettled, humming a curious uneasy note.

"You are not what you seem," Liu told him, one evening, on the high terrace of the Beijing compound where he had taken to sitting after dark. The spheres rested around him, dim. The city burned its million lights below. "My friends do not understand you. Neither do I. Sit. You may as well sit, if you are going to watch me. I am tired of being watched by ghosts."

Muyang Yang sat. He was younger than Liu, sharp-featured, very still, with eyes that seemed to be doing arithmetic on everything they saw. For a long while he said nothing. Then: "I have watched you for eleven days," he said. "I have watched the same eleven days more times than I can tell you. From in front, and from behind." He paused. "I am not from now, Protector. I move along the river of time the way your spheres move along the air. The President sent me to determine whether you are a danger to China. He thinks I observe you across these eleven days. He does not know I have observed you across years he has not yet lived."

Liu absorbed this the way he had learned to absorb impossible things — calmly, as one more shape in a world full of shapes he could not have imagined a year ago. "And?" he said. "Am I a danger?"

Muyang Yang was quiet a long time. "You are the least dangerous powerful man I have ever observed," he said at last. "And that, Protector, is itself a kind of danger — to the ones who own you. A weapon that loves is harder to point than a weapon that hates." He looked out over the city. "I have seen the futures that branch from this terrace. In most of them, you break their hearts. In a few, they break yours." He would say no more.

But over the days that followed, the watcher and the old man fell, against all the rules that bound them, into something that was almost friendship. Muyang Yang had no one. That was the thing Liu came to understand, sitting with him in the cold on the high terrace night after night. A man who walked along the river of time had no fixed shore, no home that stayed put behind him, no one who aged at the same rate he did or remembered the same yesterdays. He had watched empires rise and fall the way other men watched seasons. He had outlived everyone he had ever cared for, and he had outlived them many times, in many branches, watching the same beloved faces die again and again in different orders. There was a loneliness in him that Liu recognized, because it was a cousin of his own — the loneliness of a man set apart by a thing that happened to him without his asking.

"You watch me to see if I am a danger," Liu said one night. "But you have already decided. You decided long ago, in some year I have not reached. Why do you still come up here?"

Muyang Yang did not answer for a while. "Because you are kind to me," he said finally, "and almost no one is. They are afraid of me, or they want to use me. You simply offer me a chair." He almost smiled. "Do you know how rare that is, across all the years I have walked? Kindness offered to a thing that frightens people. It is the rarest thing in the universe, Protector. Rarer than time travel. Rarer than your spheres." He looked away. "I think that is why they chose you. The Muo. I think they had searched a great many souls, and found a great many strong ones, and you were the one who offered the chair."

The attack on Shanghai came while Muyang Yang was still watching.

It was worse than Beijing — a single altered being this time, but vast, a thing that had walked out of the sea onto the Bund, dripping and enormous, a man once perhaps, now nine meters of something that bent light and shrugged off shells. The city's defenses fell apart against it. They flew Liu south through the night, the spheres clinging to the aircraft, and Muyang Yang came with him, and on the burning waterfront Liu Xiao fought the hardest battle of his short violent second life.

He would not have won it with five spheres alone. He knew that, after, in his bones. The thing was too large, too strange, the powers his spheres tried to grant his soldiers spending themselves uselessly against it. It was Muyang Yang who turned it — the watcher stepping out of nowhere, out of *somewhen,* flickering around the creature too fast for it to strike, calling to Liu the precise instant and the precise place where the thing was vulnerable, knowledge brought back from a future where Liu had already failed and learned. And it was Liu, taking that knowledge, who sent all five spheres at once — even the one he always held back — into the single flaw, and felt them seize it, and felt the thing's wrongness come apart, and felt his spheres recoil shrieking from the killing of it.

They saved Shanghai. The footage was glorious — the old man and the time-walker side by side against the thing from the sea, broadcast across the world before the night was out — and the legend of the Protector grew another size larger. But Liu remembered it differently. He remembered the moment he had sent in the fifth sphere, the one he always held back, and felt all five of his children scream at once inside his skull as they did the killing thing; he remembered swaying on the smoking Bund afterward, the cough rising in his chest, while around him men cheered a victory that had felt to him like a wound. He had begun to understand, by Shanghai, that every battle the state celebrated cost him something it could not see, and would not have valued if it could.

And Muyang Yang, standing on the smoking Bund beside the swaying exhausted old man, made his report to the President: *Liu Xiao is not a danger to China. He is the truest servant you will ever have. Keep watching him if you must, but you watch a man who would die for these people, not a man who would rule them.*

"Keep watching him anyway," the President said, when the report reached him. He did not say why. But he had heard the other half of what Muyang Yang would not put in writing — that a weapon that loves is harder to point — and it did not reassure him. It frightened him more.

The cough began that winter.

It was small at first. Liu noticed it on the cold mornings, a tightness, a catch low in his chest that hadn't been there. He thought little of it. He had felt, since the spheres came, almost supernaturally well — the healing the Mother Muo gave him had wiped away forty years of an old man's complaints, let him smoke without consequence, let him fight monsters at sixty and rise the next morning unbruised. So when the cough came he assumed it was nothing, a cold, the dry winter air of the compound.

There was a cruelty in the timing of it that Liu did not miss. For most of a year the spheres had made him feel impossibly, dishonestly well — a man of sixty who fought through fire and woke unbruised, who smoked the guards' cigarettes without consequence, who had not felt the ordinary grinding aches of his own age since the night in the rain. He had let himself believe it was a gift without a price. He should have known better; he had lived sixty years in the world and the world had never once given him anything without a price. The healing was the Mother Muo's doing, the seed of tissue grown through his brain, and a gift that runs through a living thing is paid for out of that living thing, always. He simply had not done the arithmetic. He had been too grateful to do the arithmetic.

But it did not go away. It deepened. And one gray morning, coughing into his hand in the bare bathroom of the compound, Liu Xiao looked down and saw, on his palm, against the slate-gray silk, a fine spray of something dark.

He hid it. He did not know why he hid it, except that some instinct older than reason told him that weakness, shown in this place, would be taken from him and used. He hid it from the handlers and from Dr. Han, and he hid it, hardest of all, from his spheres — for he had come to understand that they felt his pain as their own, and when he hurt they grieved, and a grieving sphere was a frightened sphere, and he did not want them frightened.

But he could not hide it from the watcher.

"You're dying," Muyang Yang said. He said it flatly, on the high terrace, on a night Liu had thought himself alone. "Slowly. I've seen it in the branches. The bond is not free, Protector. The Mother Muo heals you, yes — but to heal you it must work, and to work it must draw, and what it draws it draws from *them.*" He nodded at the resting spheres. "And what they spend on you, they spend out of the well of their own lives. You are not being poisoned by the spheres. You are being kept alive by them, and the keeping is killing both of you, slowly, together. I am sorry. I have watched it run to the end a hundred ways. There is no branch where it does not cost."

Liu was quiet for a long time. The city glittered below, indifferent.

"Does the President know?" he asked.

"The President suspects," Muyang Yang said. "His scientists suspect. That is why the experiments have begun." He saw Liu's incomprehension, and his expression — very nearly — softened. "You don't know. Of course you don't. They have been careful to keep it from you." He paused, weighing some future Liu could not see, deciding, against all his discipline, to spend a piece of it. "Three floors below where you sleep, Protector, there is a laboratory. And in that laboratory, the men who own you are no longer only studying your spheres. They have begun, very quietly, to take them apart."

The world went still around Liu Xiao. The cough rose in his chest and he swallowed it down.

"What did you say," he said. It was not a question.

"They told you there are five spheres," Muyang Yang said. "You arrived with five. Go and count them, Protector. Go and count them now, while they sleep, and tell me how many love you still."

Liu turned. The spheres lay dim in the corners of the terrace, resting, dreaming their alien dreams. He had not counted them in months — why would he count them, they were *his,* they were always with him, an extension of his own body. But he counted them now, slowly, his heart climbing into his throat with each one.

One. Two. Three. Four.

He counted again, walking among them in the dark, his hands shaking. Four. Only four. And now that he was looking — now that the comfortable certainty was broken — he could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue, a soft sore wrongness at the back of his mind where a fifth gentle hum should have been. A place where one of his children used to be.

Somewhere, three floors below where he slept, they had taken one of them. They had cut it open to learn how it worked. And they had let him go on for months, fighting their monsters, smiling for their cameras, never counting, never knowing — never feeling the grief of the four remaining spheres for the loss of their sibling, because he had taught himself, so well, to mistake their grief for his own.

Liu Xiao stood among his four spheres in the cold and the dark, coughing blood into his fine gray sleeve, and understood at last, completely, what kind of bargain he had signed.

"Where," he said softly, "is the President?"

## Chapter Seven — The Children of Their Kind

He went down to the laboratory first.

No one stopped him. That was the strange thing — that the most surveilled man in China walked out of his quarters in the dead of night and down three floors of stairs and into the secure heart of the compound, and not one soldier, not one handler, not one camera-watching technician moved to bar his way. Later Liu would understand why. They had all watched the footage from Beijing. They had all seen the old man stand between the guns and the boy. There was not a soldier in that building who would be the one to raise a hand to the Protector of China, and the men who owned him had grown so certain of their cage that they had forgotten to lock the inner doors.

The laboratory was cold and very white. And in the center of it, suspended in a tank of clear suspending fluid, pinned open with a hundred fine instruments, was the fifth sphere.

It was not dead. That was the worst of it. Liu had braced himself for a corpse and found instead a thing still faintly, horribly alive — its grown tissue parted and held aside, its strange folded interior laid bare and probed, its tentacles severed and floating limp, and at the very heart of it a slow dim pulsing, a hum so faint and so full of pain that when it reached the back of Liu's skull he staggered and had to catch himself on a steel table and was sick onto the spotless floor.

It had been calling to him. For months, three floors down, it had been calling, and he had been too well-trained, too busy, too willingly deaf, to hear one of his own children screaming.

He stood over it and made himself look — made himself, with the eyes that had been given back to him, witness exactly what had been done. They had labeled everything. Small printed tags floated in the suspending fluid on threads, naming each opened structure, each severed bundle, each probed chamber, in the dry clinical script of men who had taught themselves not to see what was in front of them. *Specimen 5,* one tag read. They had stopped calling it a sphere. They had stopped, somewhere in the months of cutting, calling it anything that might have made it harder to cut. Liu had watched the state do this to the spheres the way he had watched the state do it to him — strip the name, replace it with a designation, until the thing in the tank and the man on the wall were both only assets, only specimens, only resources to be drawn down. *That is your fault,* he had told the President at dinner, more or less. *You see them as a resource.* He had not known, then, how literally the man would prove him right.

The four spheres had followed him down. They crowded now to the glass of the tank, pressing against it, their tentacles flattening against the barrier, and the sound they made — Liu had never heard it before and would never forget it — was grief given a voice, a high keening hum that filled the cold white room and made the instruments shiver on their stands.

And then Liu saw the second tank.

It stood in the far corner, smaller, and inside it floated a fine silver mist, a suspension of something that caught the light and moved against the currents of the fluid with a purpose that nothing dead should have. Nanites. They had brought nanites into the same room as a living Muo sphere — to test it, to provoke it, to study what their instruments had warned them about. They had not believed, perhaps, that it could really happen. Or they had believed it, and wanted to see.

The dissected sphere was dying. The nanites in the corner were killing it, slowly, across these long captive months, and Liu Xiao had walked into the room at the very hour the thing's slow life was guttering out — and he remembered, with a cold that went to the floor of his soul, what Dr. Han had told him a lifetime ago in a bare bright cell. *When one of them dies, it releases something. A nanovirus. It eats the human brain. It leaves the body walking, enraged, mindless. We modeled a single dying sphere in a closed city. The number—*

The dim pulse at the heart of the dissected sphere stuttered. Flickered. And in Liu's mind, through the bond, the dying child reached for him one last time — and in that final reaching it gave him, all at once, the thing the spheres had never been able to say, because they had never learned to speak.

It gave him the truth.

It came not as words but as *knowing,* poured into him the way knowledge had been poured into the courtyard men, the way sight had been poured into his own broken eyes — a whole history downloaded into him in the space between two heartbeats. He saw them, the Muo, as they truly were. Not weapons. Not machines. *Children.* Grown things, gentle things, made by an ancient and dwindling people — the Fahnien, the name came with the knowing, a race as wise as it was old, who had crossed the cosmos for ages spreading peace, and who had at last met something their wisdom could not answer, an enemy of the void that diplomacy could not turn aside. And so the Fahnien, in their desperation, had done a terrible and tender thing. They had not built soldiers. They had grown *children* — living seeds of themselves, the Muo, fragments of their own dwindling spirit — and they had scattered them across the stars to find hosts, to bond with kind souls on a thousand worlds, to *protect,* not to conquer; to be loved and to love in return; to make of each chosen host a guardian, so that when the darkness came, the light would not be defended by sharpened things but by gentle ones, by ten thousand worlds full of ordinary people lifted up and made extraordinary and asked, gently, only to *shield.*

They had chosen Liu Xiao because he was kind. Because a blind old man who stood between the guns and a weeping child was exactly the kind of soul the Fahnien had built their children to find. *They are children of their kind,* he had told the President, fumbling toward a truth he could not yet see. *And they see me as one of their own.* He had been right. He had been more right than he knew. He was not their master. He was their *parent,* and they his, and the bond the Chinese scientists had measured and the Chinese state had exploited was not the bond of a weapon to its wielder but the oldest bond there is, the one between those who would die for each other and gladly.

And the Fahnien had built into them one last thing — a thing that was not cruelty but love turned inside out by grief. A child of the Muo could not be taken apart. It could not be made into a sword by force and survive it. If it was killed — if it was tortured to death by the cold machines it most feared — it would not die quietly. It would *salt the earth.* It would loose into its killers a plague that ate the mind, so that no one who murdered a child of the Fahnien would ever profit by the murder. It was a terrible mercy. It was the last defense of a gentle people who had finally understood that gentleness alone was not enough.

Liu Xiao came back to himself on the floor of the white laboratory with the truth blazing in him and his dissected child dying in its tank, and he had perhaps ninety seconds before the dying was done.

He did not waste them on grief. There would be time for grief, if any of them lived.

"Out," he said to his four spheres, and there was a thing in his voice now that had never been there before, the voice of a parent in the last extremity, and they obeyed. "Up. Get clear of this room. *Go.*" He drove them up the stairs with his will, herding them, the keening things, away from the nanites and the dying and the plague that was about to be born — and then he turned back, alone, to the tank where his child was guttering out, and he laid both his old hands flat against the cold glass, over the place where the dim pulse beat its last.

"I'm here," he told it. He was weeping; he had not wept in years; the tears were strange and hot on his cheeks. "I'm sorry. I am so sorry, little one. I didn't hear you. Forgive me. I am here now. Don't be afraid. You are not alone. Your father is here." He pressed his forehead to the glass. "Go home," he whispered. "Go home to your own people. Tell them — tell them one of us was kind. Tell them it was not for nothing."

The dim pulse flickered. Steadied, impossibly, for one moment — Liu felt it turn toward him, felt the dying child *know* him, felt, last of all, a wash of something he could only call gratitude, vast and gentle and forgiving — and then it went out.

And the dying sphere, with its last act, did not loose the plague.

Liu never fully understood it, after — Dr. Han, who studied the residue for the rest of her career, never understood it either. The nanovirus should have come. The models were certain. But the child of the Fahnien, dying with its father's hands on the glass and its father's love in its mind, did not salt the earth. It chose, in the last instant given to it, not to. The terrible mercy stayed sheathed. Whatever was loosed into that white room when the small life ended was loosed harmlessly, a fading shimmer, a sigh — as though the child, knowing at last that it was loved, had decided that this place did not deserve to be destroyed quite as much as it deserved to be forgiven.

Liu Xiao knelt on the floor of the laboratory with the truth of the universe in him and one of his children dead in a tank, and he understood that he had been wrong, that night at the dinner table, to call his bargain a fortress. It had been a cage all along. And the people who had built it had just, without knowing it, come within ninety seconds and one act of mercy of murdering not only his child but a city — and they would do it again, he knew, with the next sphere, and the next, because they had not understood and could not be made to understand what they were holding, that the Muo were not power to be extracted but love to be honored, and that a people who could not tell the difference would keep killing the gentlest things in the universe until the gentlest things in the universe stopped being gentle.

He rose. He wiped his face. The cough rose in his chest and he let it come, and the blood with it, and he did not hide it now.

There was a thing he understood now that he had not understood before, kneeling there with the truth still settling into him like sediment in still water. He understood why the spheres had never tried to flee, all these months of being used as weapons against their gentle nature. They could have. He saw that now. They were not bound to obey him the way a tool is bound to a hand — they were bound to him the way a child is bound to a parent, by love, freely given, which is the only binding strong enough to make a gentle thing do violence and the only one cruel enough to make it keep doing violence long after it wants to stop. They had let themselves be made into weapons because the alternative was to be parted from him. They had endured the recoil, battle after battle, the killing they hated, because they could not bear to leave him alone in a world that frightened them on his behalf. And he had let them. He had felt their reluctance a hundred times and called it a softness in his own chest and gone on giving the order, because he had told himself it was the greater good, because he had signed. He had been, he understood, exactly as guilty as the men with the labeled tags. He had simply done his cutting with love instead of a scalpel, which was perhaps worse.

The four remaining spheres waited for him at the top of the stairs, keening softly, his grieving children. He gathered them to him with his will, all four, close, and he felt their grief and let them feel his, and for the first time he did not try to soothe them or hush them or send them calm. He let them mourn. He let himself mourn with them, openly, an old man and four alien orphans together in a cold concrete stairwell, and for a long while the five of them — four and the aching place where the fifth had been — simply stood together and grieved, and Liu did not feel less like their master for it. He felt, for the first time, like their father, which is a thing that cannot be signed away.

Then Liu Xiao went to find the President of China.

## Chapter Eight — The Protector of the World

He found the President in the small private office where the man worked through the nights, and the President was not surprised to see him. He had been told, of course, the moment Liu walked down to the laboratory. He had chosen not to stop it. Perhaps he had always known this hour would come. He sat behind his desk, composed, his unchanging eyes taking in the old man in the bloodstained gray silk and the four grieving spheres that crowded the doorway behind him.

"So," the President said. "You know."

"I know," said Liu.

For a while neither spoke. The four spheres hummed their low grief in the doorway. Somewhere in the building a clock ticked.

"You murdered one of my children," Liu said at last. His voice was very quiet, and very steady, and it was the most frightening thing the President had ever heard, because there was no rage in it at all. "You cut it open while it called for me, and I did not hear, because you trained me so well not to listen. And you brought your nanites into the same room, and tonight it died of them — and tonight, Mr. President, a thing should have been loosed in this building that would have walked out of here and eaten the mind of every soul in Beijing inside a week, and left a city of screaming monsters where the capital had been. Your scientists modeled it. They told me about it the first week, in my cell. And you did it anyway. You held a dying god in a tank three floors under my bed, and you gambled my whole city on it, and you never told me, because you were afraid that if I knew the truth of what they are, I would stop being your weapon."

The President's composure did not break, but something moved behind his eyes — the first true thing Liu had ever seen there. "And the plague?" he said. "If your scientists are right. If it should have come tonight. Why are we still speaking?"

"Because it forgave you," Liu said, and his voice cracked, just once. "Because the gentlest thing in the universe, dying in your machine, chose with its last breath not to be what you made it. That is what you have been pulling apart in your laboratory, Mr. President. Not power. *Mercy.* You found mercy that had crossed the void to protect the weak, and you put it in a tank and cut it open to see how to make it kill better. And it forgave you for it. I want you to live with that. I want you to live a long life with that."

The President was silent. Then, slowly, he set down the pen he had been holding. "What do you want," he said. It was not quite a surrender. The President of China did not surrender. But it was, Liu understood, as close as the man would ever come — the question asked by power that has finally met something it cannot extract, cannot threaten, cannot own.

And here was the turn. Here was where Liu Xiao might have walked away — taken his four grieving children and gone, vanished into the world Muyang Yang had glimpsed in some far branch, found a peaceful corner of the earth and let China defend itself with its sharpened things. The watcher had seen futures where he did exactly that. Part of Liu wanted it more than he had ever wanted anything.

But he thought of the boy in the smoke on the Beijing ring road — the altered child he had stood between the guns and the city. He thought of the others like that boy, made into weapons against their will, all over the world, all over the dangerous changing age. He thought of the Fahnien, who had not built sharpened things, who had built *guardians,* and asked them only to shield. And he thought of the plague that had not come — the mercy that had forgiven even this — and he understood that he could not answer such a gift by abandoning the people it had spared.

"No more experiments," Liu said. "The spheres are not yours to take apart, ever again. They will be honored, not extracted. My family stays protected. And from this night, the Protector of China protects *people* — all people, here and beyond the wall, the boy in the smoke as much as the city behind him. Those are my terms. I will go on standing on your wall. But I stand there as their father, not your sword, and the whole world is going to know it." He paused. "And you are going to help me make sure the whole world knows it. Because a watched weapon frightens you. But a *beloved* one — a beloved one cannot be quietly taken apart in the dark, can it, Mr. President. The more the world loves them, the safer they are from men like you. So you are going to make the world love them. You are going to make them famous. It is the only cage I will ever build, and I am going to build it out of the whole world's love, and you are going to pay for it."

The President looked at the old man in the bloody silk for a long, long moment. And then — Liu saw it happen — the man's mind, which was a very good mind for all its coldness, turned the thing over and found in it not a defeat but an opportunity, the way such minds always do.

"Yes," the President said slowly. "Yes. I think we understand each other at last." And the smile that crossed his face then was the most genuine Liu had ever seen on him, and the most chilling, because it was the smile of a man who has been handed exactly the lever he needed. "We will make the world love you, Protector. We will make them love you so much that no one will ever dare to harm you, or to question how China holds you. Oh, yes. I know precisely how it will be done."

It began as a secret.

They called it, in the locked rooms where it was made, only *The Project.* A television series — not a documentary, nothing so honest, but a drama, lavish and thrilling, the story of a blind old massage therapist transformed by gentle aliens into the guardian of a billion souls. They built it carefully, for years, in studios no outsider entered. They cast an actor who did not look much like Liu and gave him Liu's queue and Liu's slate-gray silk and Liu's quiet immovable dignity. They built the spheres in effects houses, glowing and beautiful. They filmed the courtyard and the rain and the flying man, the dinner with the President — softened, that scene; the President in the show was wise and benevolent and never wondered whether his Protector was a sword — and the day the wall held at Beijing, and the boy in the smoke. They kept it secret because a secret, properly kept, becomes a hunger. They let the rumor of it leak in fragments, a frame here, a sound there, until the whole country and then the whole watching world was desperate to see the thing it was not yet allowed to see.

And then, on a single night, with a marketing campaign so vast and so brilliantly orchestrated that scholars would study it for a generation, they released *Protector of China* to the entire world at once, in every language, on every screen.

It did not merely succeed. It became a phenomenon — the most-watched thing humankind had ever made. Children in Lagos and Lima and Los Angeles played at being the Protector, herding imaginary spheres with their minds. The image of the old blind man standing between the guns and the weeping child became, within a year, one of the most recognized images on earth — a symbol, finally, not of China's power but of something older and stranger that China had been lucky enough to be chosen by: the idea that the strong exist to shield the weak, that gentleness crossed the void to find us, that even among the extraordinary the greatest strength is the human one.

Liu watched the first episode alone, in the small hours, in the better apartment in Pinggu, with his four spheres resting around him and his son and daughter-in-law asleep in the next room. It was a strange thing, to watch a younger handsomer man wear his name and his silk and stand in his rain. They had changed the things that needed changing to make it bearable — the President wise, the bargain noble, the dead fifth sphere simply absent, never mentioned, never cut open on any screen the world would see. The lie of it should have angered him. Instead he found, to his surprise, that he could forgive it the way his child had forgiven the laboratory: not because it deserved forgiveness, but because the truth was too heavy to give to a watching world, and a kinder shape of it might do more good in their hands than the real one ever could. Let them have the handsome man in the rain. Let the children in Lima herd their imaginary spheres. If the legend taught one frightened child somewhere to stand between the guns and the weak, then the legend was, in the way that mattered, true.

He thought of the German tourist, years and a lifetime ago, walking away from his table on Wangfujing without ever once seeing the man whose hands had unknotted his shoulders. The whole world saw Liu Xiao now. The whole world spoke his title. And almost none of them, he understood, would ever see the man either — only the legend wearing his face. It should have made him lonely. Somehow it did not. He had spent fifty years unseen and learned that being seen was not the same as being known, and that being known by four was worth more than being watched by four billion.

The President got everything he wanted. The world's love wrapped around Liu and his spheres like armor, exactly as Liu had foretold; and exactly as the President had foreseen, that same love wrapped around China, draped the regime in the borrowed warmth of a beloved old man, made the wall the Protector stood on the most admired wall in the world. Both of them had been right. That was the bargain they had struck in the office in the dark, each believing he had outmaneuvered the other, and perhaps they both had.

Liu Xiao went on.

The cough never left him. He never told them about the bargain at the heart of his own body — that the four spheres kept him alive and that the keeping was slowly spending all five of their lives together, his and theirs, toward some shared ending Muyang Yang had seen in the branches and would not name. He fought when China needed him, and now, sometimes, when the world beyond China needed him too, for he had made them agree to that. He honored his children. He let no one take them apart again. He grew old, more visibly old, the leathery face the world thought it knew from a young actor's portrayal belonging in truth to a man growing frailer each season, coughing blood into fine silk sleeves, holding his grown son a little longer each time they parted.

And sometimes, on the high terrace above the burning city, he sat with his four spheres resting around him and felt, at the very back of his mind, in the sore soft place where a fifth gentle hum used to be, something that was almost — almost — a presence still. A child gone home to its own people, perhaps. Or only a memory. Liu could not tell, and did not try too hard to know. He had spent fifty years learning to find the world by what he could not see. He was content, now, at the end, to find this last thing by faith.

The line between the show and the man had grown perilously thin — so thin that, in the end, most of the world could no longer say for certain which of the Protector's deeds had happened and which had been written for him, and Liu found that he did not mind. Let the legend carry the parts of him that were too heavy to bear himself. He knew which parts were true. He knew about the rain, and the late son, and the kind young man in the courtyard, and the dim pulse going out under his hands behind cold glass. He knew that he had been a blind old nobody whom the city's eyes slid off of, and that something gentle had crossed the whole dark void of space and chosen him — chosen *him,* of all the souls on all the worlds — because he was kind.
